# Short-task biology benchmarks for a team of agents

Scout: scout-short. Date: Saturday 14 November 2026. Research used 40 calls to `lab/tools/exa.py` (logged in `records/exa_calls.jsonl`). No repositories were cloned, no data downloaded, no benchmark code run, and no answer files opened.

The question: where can our team of agents realistically beat the best published single-model score this weekend, on a laptop (8 cores, 16 GB)?

The short answer: very few of these benchmarks have scores from late-2026 frontier models. The ones that do (GeneBench-Pro, LABBench2) are either small in their public part or partly saturated. Most of the others were last scored with 2024 or 2025 models, so a "win" there means little unless we also run a current single-model baseline ourselves with the same model, the same tools and the same budget. That is probably the honest design anyway.

## GeneBench-Pro (OpenAI, June 2026)

1. **Task.** Each problem gives the agent a messy synthetic dataset, a short experimental context and a target number tied to a decision (for example a GWAS meta-analysis with QC and sample overlap). The agent must explore the data, run QC, pick a method, run it, check diagnostics and return an exact JSON answer. 129 problems in 10 domains: statistical genetics 17, population genetics 21, quantitative genetics 17, regulatory omics 17, functional genomics 9, proteomics 7, clinical and pharmacogenomics 26, cancer genomics 10, microbial 3, forensic 2. Source: https://openai.com/index/introducing-genebench-pro/
2. **Grading.** Deterministic. A problem passes only when every graded field matches exactly or falls within a numeric tolerance. Data are simulated from a known causal model, so the answer is known and the authors checked that plausible wrong analyses fail. Sources: OpenAI page above; https://spectrai-initiative.github.io/bio-benchmark-atlas/benchmarks/genebench-pro/
3. **Access.** Split into 10 public problems, 50 given to Artificial Analysis, and 69 internal holdout. The 10 public problems ship with prompts, data, ground truth, grader configuration and reports on Hugging Face. Licence is conflicted (dataset card says CC BY 4.0, root LICENSE says MIT). Leak risk: the ground truth sits in the same public package, so our agents must never see those files. Source: BioBench Atlas page above.
4. **Compute.** Isolated workspace with Python, scientific libraries and PLINK 2.0; no domain-specific tools needed. Data size and run time per problem: not found. Reviewers estimate a human expert needs 20-40 hours per problem. Source: OpenAI page.
5. **Scores (full 129, current frontier).** GPT-5.6 Sol 28.7% at max reasoning, 31.5% with Pro mode. GPT-5.2 4.9%, GPT-5.4 8.9%, GPT-5.5 12.0%, GPT-5.6 Luna 16.5%, GPT-5.6 Terra 23.3%. Claude Opus 4.8 (high) 9%, with 78.3% of problems at 0% pass. Ten attempts per problem for most models, five for Claude Opus and GPT Pro. OpenAI says the benchmark "may be saturated by the end of the year." Sources: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.06.29.735386v2.full and the BioBench Atlas page. Per-problem scores on the 10 public problems: not found. Without them we cannot compare our team with the published numbers on the same tasks; we would have to run the single-model baseline ourselves.
6. **Why agents fail.** "In many failures, the agent notices the relevant local diagnostic clue but treats it as a local data cleaning issue" instead of changing the downstream method (bioRxiv paper, Results).

Judgement: (a) Yes, 10 problems with several attempts each fit in 12 hours, if data are small (unverified). (b) Large headroom: even the best model is below a third. (c) Yes. The named failure is a reasoning-chain error that a critic agent asking "does this diagnostic change the method?" could catch. The weakness is n=10, so differences will be noisy.

## GenoTEX, and the GenoMAS team result

1. **Task.** From raw GEO or TCGA gene expression files, select datasets, preprocess them (map probes to genes, pull clinical traits from free-text metadata) and find genes associated with a trait, optionally adjusting for a condition. 1,384 problems (132 unconditional, 1,252 conditional) over 911 datasets. Source: https://github.com/Liu-Hy/GenoTEX
2. **Grading.** Automatic: F1 and AUROC of the predicted significant genes against expert-curated lists; preprocessing is scored by Composite Similarity Correlation against expert tables. Source: https://dream.ischool.illinois.edu/blogs/ai_agents_gene_expression_analysis.html
3. **Access.** GitHub plus Google Drive, Baidu, Kaggle and Hugging Face. Licence listed as "Other" on GitHub; a third-party summary says CC BY 4.0 (https://jmchilton.github.io/statistical-genomics-foundry/design/project-genotex/). Leak risk is high: the expert outputs (`output/regress/.../significant_genes_*.json`) come in the same download as the inputs. We would have to split them before any agent sees the folder.
4. **Compute.** 41.5 GB of raw input; 82 GB total. About 167 samples and 18,530 genes per dataset on average. A subset of a few traits should fit a laptop; regression at this size is light. Time per problem: not found. GenoMAS cost $0.38 per problem in API fees.
5. **Scores.** GenoMAS (team of six; Claude Sonnet 4 writes code, o3 plans and reviews, Gemini 2.5 Pro is the domain expert) 60.48% F1. Same team, all Claude Sonnet 4: 52.98%. All o3: 45.53%. GenoAgent (earlier team) 43.63%. Biomni (single general biomedical agent) 14.82%. o3 naming genes without data 5.13%. Human experts 71.63%. Paper is ACL Findings 2026; the models are from 2025. Source: DREAM Lab blog above (Table 1 of arXiv 2507.21035).
6. **Why agents fail.** The bottleneck is clinical metadata: gene tables match experts at 91.15% similarity, clinical trait tables at only 32.61%. General agents (AutoGen, MetaGPT, Claude Code, Cursor, Manus) did not produce complete valid analyses on three hypertension cohorts; they skipped probe-to-gene mapping or misread how cohorts encoded disease subtypes. Source: DREAM Lab blog.

Judgement: (a) Yes for a subset of 2-5 traits, after a careful data split. (b) Unknown against a late-2026 single model, because nobody has published one. The gap to human experts (about 11 points) is real. (c) Yes: removing the Code Reviewer cut F1 from 60.48% to 24.98%, the largest drop of any ablation. This is the clearest published evidence that checking each other's work helps on biology analysis.

## ScienceAgentBench (bioinformatics and computational chemistry tasks)

1. **Task.** Write one self-contained Python program that processes the given data and saves results (for example, train a cell-counting model on BBBC002, or a toxicity model on ClinTox). 102 tasks from 44 papers in four disciplines. Bioinformatics 27, computational chemistry 20, GIS 27, psychology and cognitive neuroscience 28 (counts from a secondary summary citing the authors' dataset: https://github.com/yuema137/scientific-eval-environments/blob/main/zh/works/scienceagentbench.md). Sources: https://osu-nlp-group.github.io/ScienceAgentBench/
2. **Grading.** Success Rate: each task has an evaluation program written during annotation (test-set performance, answer matches, figure quality). Figures are judged by GPT-4o, so an OpenAI key is needed. A "Verified" split released 30 April 2026 fixes false negatives. Source: https://github.com/AK2000/ScienceAgentBench (fork of the OSU repo, same README).
3. **Access.** Inputs on Hugging Face (`osunlp/ScienceAgentBench`). The full benchmark (datasets, eval programs, gold programs, rubrics) is a zip with a password published in the README. Leak risk: gold programs are inside that zip, so agents must run without access to it. MIT licence. Also packaged for OpenHands and Harbor.
4. **Compute.** Containerized evaluation runs all 102 tasks in about 30 minutes with 8 threads (README). Some tasks train small deep models; CPU-only time per task: not found.
5. **Scores.** HAL leaderboard (paused): o3 Medium 33.33%; Claude Sonnet 4.5 High, Claude 3.7 Sonnet High and GPT-5 Medium 30.39%; Claude Opus 4.1 27.45%. All 2025 models. Source: https://hal.cs.princeton.edu/scienceagentbench. Mimosa (March 2026) with DeepSeek-V3.2: 43.1% (see team section). Source: https://github.com/HolobiomicsLab/Mimosa-AI. Scores for late-2026 frontier models: not found.
6. **Why agents fail.** Not extracted this session (paper not read in full).

Judgement: (a) Yes, all 47 bio and chem tasks with a few attempts fit in 12 hours. (b) Likely headroom, but unproven for late-2026 models; run our own baseline. (c) Partly: Mimosa shows a team can beat a single agent on this benchmark, and that a naive team can lose.

## LABBench2 (Edison Scientific, February 2026)

1. **Task.** About 1,900 tasks in 11 families. The code-and-data parts: SeqQA2 (400 tasks, 20 sequence-analysis templates x 20 variants, sequences given inline, as a file, or to be retrieved) and CloningQA (14 end-to-end cloning protocol designs). Sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09554, https://github.com/EdisonScientific/labbench2
2. **Grading.** SeqQA2 and CloningQA use a deterministic scorer; LitQA3, DbQA2, FigQA2 and ProtocolQA2 use an LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 4.5 in the Inspect port). Source: https://ukgovernmentbeis.github.io/inspect_evals/evals/lab_bench_2/index.html
3. **Access.** Dataset `futurehouse/labbench2` on Hugging Face; public harness. Whether answers are in the public dataset: not checked; assume yes and guard.
4. **Compute.** Light, except 100 SeqQA2 tasks that need >600k tokens of sequence. CloningQA with tools took GPT-5.2 1h41m for 14 tasks (Inspect report).
5. **Scores.** Inspect report, June 2026, GPT-5.2: CloningQA (inject, tools) 28.6%; SeqQA2 (retrieve, bare) 11.5%; LitQA3 (tools) 79.9%. At release, frontier models scored at most about 50% on SeqQA2. An audit by Generality Labs (29 September 2026) found that 25% of SeqQA2 tasks are hard mainly from resource limits, 11% have unclear prompts and 8% are flawed; after fixes, pass rates rise to 86-90%, so SeqQA2 is "functionally saturated". Source: https://generality.org/blog/posts/labbench2-seqqa2-saturated/
6. **Why agents fail.** Exact string fidelity on long sequences and using tools correctly (paper, Discussion).

Judgement: (a) CloningQA yes (14 tasks). (b) CloningQA has headroom (28.6%) but n=14 is tiny; SeqQA2 has none. (c) Partly: a checker that re-verifies sequences with code could help on cloning.

## BioDSBench and BioDSA-1K (Zifeng Wang, Jimeng Sun)

**BioDSBench.** 293 coding tasks (128 Python, 165 R) from 39 studies across 7 biomedical areas, run on cBioPortal and UCSC Xena tables. Graded by test cases (Pass@1). Reference answers and test cases are public on Hugging Face (`zifeng-ai/BioDSBench`), a leak risk. Best: DSWizard agent "up to 0.74 Pass@1" (https://biodsa.github.io/); the paper tested 2024 models (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 1.5) (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41551-025-01587-2, Nature Biomedical Engineering 2026). Laptop friendly. Headroom against a current model: likely small, not measured.

**BioDSA-1K.** 1,029 hypotheses from 329 papers, with cBioPortal data. The agent writes code and decides True, False or Not verifiable; scored on Type I and Type II error, evidence alignment, executability and spotting non-verifiable claims. Labels are in the public parquet (leak risk). Agents tested: CodeGen and ReAct with GPT-4o and o3-mini (May 2025). Type II errors (missed findings) were higher than Type I, up to 0.191 in some categories; evidence alignment only 0.20-0.25; ReAct code ran 84.9-86.6% of the time. Agents were "conservative". Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2505.16100. The harness (BioDSA framework) is open: https://github.com/Keiji-AI/BioDSA

Judgement: (a) Yes, both are cheap. (b) Unknown for 2026 models; only old models published. (c) The low evidence-alignment scores suggest a reviewer could help, but beating 2025 GPT-4o proves little.

## BioML-bench

1. **Task.** End-to-end biomedical ML: read a task, build a pipeline, train, and submit predictions. 24 tasks in the paper set (imaging 4, drug discovery 9, protein engineering 6, single-cell 5); 80+ Polaris drug-discovery tasks available. Sources: https://github.com/science-machine/biomlbench, https://autoscientists.openscientist.ai/
2. **Grading.** Hidden test labels; reported as leaderboard percentile vs human submissions, above-median rate and medal rate.
3. **Access.** MIT, v0.1-alpha ("expect bugs"). Built on MLE-bench; data downloaded per task. A 22-task Harbor package exists: https://hub.harborframework.com/datasets/nextflow-frontier/bioml-bench
4. **Compute.** "Several GB" for the full set; needs Docker. GPU need and time per task: not found; imaging tasks likely slow on CPU.
5. **Scores.** Original paper (bioRxiv, September 2025): four open-source agents (STELLA, Biomni, AIDE, MLAgentBench) underperform human baselines on average. AutoScientists (decentralized team): 74.4% mean leaderboard percentile, "+8.33%" over the strongest prior biomedical agent. Sources: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.01.673319v3 and the AutoScientists page.
6. **Why agents fail.** Agents that tried more diverse ML strategies scored higher; scaffolding mattered more than biomedical specialization (paper abstract).

Judgement: (a) Only a few drug-discovery tasks would fit. (b) Some headroom. (c) Yes in principle (AutoScientists), but the work is long training runs, not short analyses.

## BLADE, DiscoveryBench, DSBench, BioCoder, BioAgent Bench

- **BLADE.** 12 research questions and datasets with over 500 expert analysis decisions; 4 are biology or ecology, none omics. Best about 44.8% F1, 2024 models. Sources: https://blade-bench.github.io/, https://spectrai-initiative.github.io/bio-benchmark-atlas/benchmarks/blade/. Too little biology.
- **DiscoveryBench.** 264 real tasks (25 train, 239 test) in sociology, biology, humanities, economics, engineering and meta-science, plus 903 synthetic tasks. Graded by a GPT-4-based Hypothesis Match Score. Peak 25% with 2024 models. Number of biology tasks: not found. Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01725
- **DSBench.** 466 analysis and 74 modeling tasks from ModelOff and Kaggle. Best agent 34.12% in 2024 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07703); OpenAI reported ChatGPT agent above human level in July 2025. No biology part found.
- **BioCoder.** Bioinformatics code generation from GitHub functions and Rosalind problems (Bioinformatics 2024). Its grader runs on AWS Lambda, SQS, DynamoDB and EC2; the README says a local version will come "in the future". Scores are GPT-4 era. Source: https://github.com/gersteinlab/BioCoder. Skip.
- **BioAgent Bench.** 10 end-to-end pipelines (RNA-seq, variant calling, metagenomics), LLM grader, tasks capped at 4 hours and 48 GB RAM. Claude Opus 4.5 reached 100% completion; Gemini 3 Pro, GPT-5.2 and Sonnet 4.5 exceeded 90%. Under perturbations, GPT-5.2 spotted corrupted inputs in 7 of 10 tasks and used decoy files in 2 of 10, and prompt bloat cut completion by 28%. Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.21800. Saturated on the main metric and too heavy for 16 GB; the perturbation idea is worth copying.

## Teams of agents versus single agents: what is published

1. **Mimosa on ScienceAgentBench, same model (DeepSeek-V3.2), March 2026.** Single agent 38.2% at $0.05 per task. One-shot multi-agent 32.4% at $0.38: **worse than the single agent**. Iterative-learning multi-agent (workflows refined from feedback) 43.1% at $1.70, about 34 times the single agent's cost. The authors also report that iterative learning helps GPT-4o but slightly hurts Claude Haiku 4.5. Sources: https://github.com/HolobiomicsLab/Mimosa-AI, https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28986
2. **GenoMAS on GenoTEX (ACL Findings 2026).** Not a same-model single-agent comparison, but the ablations isolate the checking: removing the Code Reviewer cut F1 from 60.48% to 24.98%; one review round instead of several gave 46.61%; removing the Domain Expert gave 47.57%. Mixing models (60.48%) beat the all-Claude-Sonnet-4 team (52.98%) at half the cost. General single coding agents, including Claude Code, did not complete valid analyses on three test cohorts. Source: DREAM Lab blog above.
3. **"Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems" (Google Research and MIT, 2026).** A controlled study across 260 configurations and six agentic benchmarks (not biology), with prompts, tools and budgets held equal. Two cautions for us: tool-heavy tasks suffer from coordination overhead, and "tasks where single-agent performance already exceeds 45% accuracy experience negative returns from additional agents." Multi-agent setups gained +81% on Finance-Agent and lost 70% on PlanCraft. Sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296, https://research.google/blog/towards-a-science-of-scaling-agent-systems-when-and-why-agent-systems-work/, https://www.media.mit.edu/projects/towards-a-science-of-scaling-agent-systems-when-and-why-agent-systems-work/overview/
4. **AutoScientists on BioML-bench (2026).** Under matched budgets, a decentralized team beat prior agents (74.4% mean percentile). Removing the three analyst agents dropped TDC-hERG AUROC from 0.867 to 0.738. The comparison is with other systems, not the same model run as one agent. Source: https://autoscientists.openscientist.ai/

What this means for us: a team helps when it adds real checking (a reviewer that re-runs code or questions a method) and when the single model is well below about 45%. A team that only splits the work into hand-offs can do worse than one agent. GeneBench-Pro (best single model under a third) and GenoTEX both sit in the zone where a team should help. ScienceAgentBench has published evidence in both directions.
